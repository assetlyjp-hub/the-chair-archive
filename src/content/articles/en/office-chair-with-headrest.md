"Should I get a chair with a headrest, or skip it?" It's one of the last decisions buyers wrestle with. The honest answer: **a headrest is essential for some workflows and pure dead weight for others**. This guide walks through who actually benefits, who doesn't, why aftermarket headrests usually disappoint, which built-in models are worth your money, and what to check before pulling the trigger.

## When a Headrest Genuinely Helps

A headrest exists to transfer the weight of your head — roughly 10 to 12 pounds for adults — from your neck muscles to the chair. That matters in specific situations:

- **You're on calls and video meetings for hours.** Reclining slightly while listening, with your head supported, lets the trapezius and cervical muscles disengage. Most people don't realize how much they tense those muscles until they finally rest them.
- **You recline often during the day.** If you tilt back to think, read, or take a breather every 30 minutes, the difference between a supported head and a floating one compounds across an 8-hour day.
- **You have a history of neck pain or "tech neck."** A headrest physically discourages forward head posture, which is the single biggest contributor to chronic cervical strain.
- **You're tall (5'9" / 175cm and above).** Standard backrests cap out around your shoulders, leaving your neck unsupported even when reclining. A headrest closes that gap.

## When a Headrest Is Wasted

Skip the headrest entirely if any of these describe you:

- **You do deep focus work all day.** Coders, writers, designers, and engineers spend hours leaning slightly forward toward a monitor. Your head never touches the headrest.
- **You almost never recline.** If you sit at 90 degrees and stay there, the headrest is just a visual obstruction.
- **Your monitor is close and you scrutinize it.** Forward head movement pulls you completely off the headrest.
- **You're shorter than 5'3" (160cm).** Fixed headrests often hit the base of the skull instead of the back of the head, which actively creates strain.

## The Three Big Problems with Aftermarket Headrests

"I'll just bolt one onto my current chair." Tempting, but here's why most aftermarket headrests fail in practice:

### 1. Alignment is almost always wrong

Backrests are engineered with specific curves and rigidity, none of which assume head support. Clamping a third-party headrest on top forces your neck into a position that **breaks the natural alignment between your spine and skull**. It can feel fine for the first hour and trigger neck pain by hour four.

### 2. Durability is poor

Most aftermarket units mount via clamps, friction pads, or screw holes that weren't designed to handle leverage. The torque from reclining works the joint loose. **Six to twelve months in, wobble sets in** — and a wobbling headrest is worse than no headrest at all.

### 3. They don't track recline

A built-in headrest is engineered to follow the recline curve so your neck angle stays natural through the full tilt range. Aftermarket headrests are fixed-angle, so the more you recline, the more they push or float relative to your head.

**Bottom line: if you actually want a headrest, buy a chair with one built in.**

## Built-In Headrest Recommendations by Price Tier

### Entry to mid-range: SIHOO Doro C300 / S300

Around $300–500. Comes with **4D armrests, lumbar support, and an adjustable headrest as standard equipment**. Adjustment range is generous — height, tilt, and depth — making it the easiest entry point for trying a built-in headrest without a thousand-dollar commitment.

### Mid-range: Branch Verve

Popular in the US for its **wide range of upholstery colors** and clean home-office aesthetics. The headrest is a fixed-offset design, so adjustment is limited, but the visual finish punches above its price.

### High-end: Ergohuman Pro / Elite II

Roughly $1,200–1,800. Every contact point — **headrest, lumbar, and armrests — is multi-axis adjustable**. Recline tracking is excellent, which is why it has such a strong reputation among people who live on video calls.

### Important: Aeron and Gesture have no factory option

The [Herman Miller Aeron](/articles/en/herman-miller-aeron-review-2026) — arguably the most iconic office chair ever made — has **no manufacturer-supplied headrest**. None. Third-party units exist, but they fall into all three aftermarket traps above. The Steelcase Gesture is the same story: no official headrest variant. If a headrest is non-negotiable, choose a different chair rather than modifying these.

## Adjustable vs. Fixed Headrests

| Type | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Fully adjustable (height, tilt, depth) | Shared chairs, users outside 5'7"–5'10", frequent recliners | More mechanisms means more potential for lock slippage over time |
| Fixed offset shape | Solo users in average height range, minimalist aesthetic | If your body doesn't fit the fixed geometry, there's nowhere to go |

**When in doubt, pick adjustable.** A non-adjustable headrest that doesn't fit your body has no escape hatch.

## Honest Downsides

- **You pay more.** The headrest variant of any chair is typically $100–300 above the base model.
- **Footprint grows.** Pushing the chair against a wall is harder.
- **Head-mobility tasks suffer.** Musicians, multi-monitor users, and anyone who frequently rotates their head can find the headrest in the way.
- **Visual weight.** A headrest reads as "executive" and can look out of place in a clean, minimalist room.

## FAQ

### Q1. Are headrests good for posture?
They help when you recline but **encourage slouching when used upright**. Treat the headrest as a tool you engage during recline, not a default resting position.

### Q2. Do headrests fix forward head posture / "tech neck"?
They don't cure it, but they **physically discourage the head from drifting forward**, which slows progression. Combine with monitor height adjustment for actual results.

### Q3. What's the difference between a gaming chair headrest and an ergonomic one?
Gaming chairs use **pillow-style cushions at fixed height**, optimized for short-term comfort. Ergonomic chairs use **shaped shells with adjustment mechanisms** designed for 8-hour days. For work, choose ergonomic.

### Q4. Is anyone happy with aftermarket headrests?
Light users — people who recline only a few times a week — can get by. Daily, multi-hour recliners should always go built-in.

### Q5. Where should the headrest actually sit?
**Mid-back-of-skull, just above ear level.** If it's pressing the base of your skull or upper neck, it's too low and will create the exact tension you're trying to avoid.

### Q6. Worth it if I rarely recline fully?
Possibly. Even **micro-breaks** — closing your eyes for 60 seconds, looking up during a call — let you decompress neck muscles you didn't know were tight.

### Q7. I own an Aeron and now I want a headrest. What do I do?
**Sell the Aeron and buy a different chair.** Aftermarket Aeron headrests require modifications that void any warranty conversation, and they tank resale value. Aerons hold price extremely well on the used market, so the swap is cheaper than people expect.

## The Bottom Line

A headrest isn't universally better — it's **dramatically better for recline-heavy workflows and irrelevant for forward-focused work**. Don't try to bolt one on after the fact; the alignment, durability, and recline-tracking issues are real. Decide first whether your work style actually needs head support, then buy a chair that was engineered for it from day one.
